There's a version of career advice that has dominated the tech industry for a decade. Get deep. Niche down. Become the world's best at one specific thing. The T-shaped professional. The expert who earns a premium.

I understand the logic. It's good advice for large organizations with rigid role definitions. It's how you get promoted in a company where dozens of people need to coordinate. It's how you become legible to a system that sorts people into boxes.

It's mostly wrong for the kind of work I do.

## What "generalist" actually means

People use the word generalist to mean someone who knows a bit about many things — which implies, if you read between the lines, that they don't know enough about any of them.

That's not what I mean.

The generalists I respect aren't people with shallow knowledge spread wide. They're people with deep knowledge in multiple domains that feed into each other. A designer who understands engineering constraints. A developer who understands copywriting. A product person who understands distribution. A builder who can hold the entire system in their head and make decisions that account for all of it.

This is a different capability than specialization. It's not harder or easier — it's genuinely different, and it's genuinely rare.

## The assembly line problem

Most agency and freelance work is structured like an assembly line. Brief to strategist. Strategy to designer. Design to developer. Developer to client. Each handoff loses information. Each specialist optimizes for their piece without full context.

The brief becomes a game of telephone. The developer builds exactly what the designer specified, even when the designer made an assumption that a developer would immediately recognize as expensive. The designer produces pixel-perfect screens that the developer can't replicate in a real browser without major compromises. The strategist recommends an approach that looks good on paper and falls apart in implementation.

I've worked inside this system and outside it. Outside it is better.

When one person can hold the whole problem — understand the brief, design the solution, build the implementation, and communicate with the client — the feedback loops collapse. A decision that would take two weeks to cycle through an agency's process takes a day. A mistake gets caught before it compounds. The result is better and cheaper and faster.

This is not about being cheaper than an agency. It's about removing the translation cost.

## The AI angle

Here's where it gets interesting.

The tools that are changing fastest are the ones that were the hardest bottlenecks for non-specialists. A designer who couldn't code can now produce a working prototype with AI assistance. A developer who couldn't write can now produce serviceable copy. A founder who couldn't do either can now get something functional in front of users.

This should terrify narrow specialists. And it does.

But it doesn't threaten the person who already operates across those domains with genuine depth. It accelerates them. The designer-developer doesn't need AI to bridge the gap between design and code — they can already bridge it. AI gives them speed on the rote work so they can spend more time on the judgment work.

The generalist has always held an advantage that's hard to articulate: they catch problems before they become problems, because they can see the system whole. AI makes that advantage more pronounced, not less. The person who understands both the design and the implementation can use AI tools in both domains. The specialist who only understands one is still dependent on someone else for the other — except now that "someone else" is a language model that sometimes hallucinates.

## What the internet gets wrong

The internet's take on career building is skewed by what the internet can observe. Specialists produce content that's legible and searchable. "I'm the Webflow developer for B2B SaaS landing pages" generates a clean personal brand, a specific audience, a clear value prop. "I do design, development, strategy, and AI integrations across a range of industries" generates a LinkedIn profile that looks unfocused.

The results tell a different story.

The most capable people I've worked with — the ones I wanted on my side when things got complicated — were generalists. Not because depth doesn't matter. But because they had depth in more than one thing, and they could see how those things connected.

The market doesn't always reward that directly. Clients sometimes don't know to ask for it. But when a project needs someone who can hold the whole problem, the generalist is not a compromise. They're the right choice.

## The compounding advantage

Specializations are brittle. The skill that's in demand today is commoditized in three years and automated in five. The generalist who keeps adding genuine competencies — not shallow exposure, but real depth — builds a portfolio of capabilities that compounds.

It doesn't matter that no single skill dominates. What matters is that the combination does. And combinations are hard to replicate.

When I look at my own work — Webflow builds, Framer sites, AI integrations, product strategy, SEO architecture — none of those are islands. They feed each other. A Webflow project benefits from understanding SEO. An AI integration benefits from understanding design. A product strategy benefits from understanding what's actually buildable.

The generalist is playing a different game than the specialist. The specialist is trying to be the best at the thing. The generalist is trying to understand the system that the thing lives inside.

Both are legitimate. But in a world where tools are getting better at executing the thing, understanding the system is becoming more valuable, not less.

I'd rather understand the system.
