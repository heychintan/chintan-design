Every few months the conversation resets. A new model drops. Someone shares a screenshot of a UI generated in thirty seconds. The thread fills with a mix of awe and anxiety. "Is this the end of design?"

It isn't. But the people asking the question are often missing the more interesting one: what does this expose about how most designers have been working?

## The automation anxiety is aimed at the wrong thing

The work that AI is replacing right now is work that probably shouldn't have been done by skilled designers in the first place.

Resizing assets for different breakpoints. Generating twelve variations of a button state. Writing boilerplate component documentation. Creating placeholder screens for developer handoffs. Low-fidelity wireframes that communicate spatial logic without communicating anything else.

This is rote work. It's necessary, but it's not design. Design is the judgment that precedes the execution. The question of *why* before the answer of *how*. AI can produce a layout. It cannot decide what the layout should make the person feel, or what it should make them do next.

When a builder tells me they're worried about AI taking their job, I ask them to describe what they do on a typical day. If most of the answer is "making things that were already decided," that's a real concern. If most of the answer is "deciding what to make and why," that's a moat.

## The exposure

Here's the uncomfortable part: a lot of "design" work has always been execution dressed up as thinking.

An experienced designer could always produce a credible-looking screen in Figma. A landing page that follows conventions, hits the visual beats, uses the right component patterns. Clients would nod. Developers would build. Everyone moves on.

AI can now do that too. In thirty seconds. For free.

What it can't do is understand context deeply enough to break conventions intentionally. It can't have the conversation with a founder where you push back on the brief because the real problem isn't what they described. It can't feel the friction in an onboarding flow and know that the issue isn't the UI — it's the mental model the product is asking users to adopt.

AI produces locally coherent output. It doesn't have a globally coherent perspective on the product. That perspective — that is design.

## What gets more valuable

The skills that compound in this new environment are not technical. They're judgment-based.

**Understanding why something doesn't work** — not just that it doesn't. Anyone can point at a low-converting page. The designer who can trace the failure to a specific copywriting decision, or a misaligned audience assumption, or a product positioning problem — that's the person who gets called back.

**Knowing which constraints actually matter** — scope, budget, timeline, technical debt, team capability. A generated design has no constraints. Real products are built inside them. The designer who works fluently inside constraints produces better outcomes than the designer who ignores them.

**The conversation itself** — clients hire designers in part because they want someone who sees things they don't. A chatbot produces output. A designer produces perspective. These are different products.

## The shape of what's coming

I think the next five years will be hard for people in the middle. Too experienced to compete on speed, not senior enough to compete on judgment. AI handles the commodity work from below; senior people handle the strategic work from above.

The response isn't to work faster or produce more. It's to go deeper. To be the person who brings clarity to a problem before anyone starts building anything.

For web development specifically — Webflow, Framer, the no-code space — I think AI actually expands the addressable market rather than shrinking it. More people will have AI-generated sites, and more of them will hit the ceiling of what AI can produce. They'll need someone who knows when to use the tool and when to override it. Someone who can tell the difference between a site that looks good and a site that works.

That someone still needs to exist. That someone still needs to be thoughtful.

## The honest question

If AI can do what you do, the question isn't "what do I do now?" The question is: "was what I was doing actually the thing that created value?"

For most skilled designers who've been treating craft as strategy, the answer is yes. The work is harder to articulate than "I made this look good," but it's real and it compounds.

For everyone coasting on the gap between taste and tools — that gap is closing. The tools are catching up to taste. What remains is judgment. And judgment takes years, not prompts.
